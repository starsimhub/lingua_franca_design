# LASER England and Wales measles, in Starsim

The translation of [examples/laser/england_wales](../../../examples/laser/england_wales), LASER's model for wave 3. The model is `model.py`; `compare.py` checks it against the original's reference results. Both run the same scaled-down version of the notebook's model: the 53 places within 100 km of Norwich, at their real sizes, for 10 years (see the example's README for why).

## Comparison

| Measure | Original | Starsim | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lines of model code | 126 | 62 | 12 lines are over 100 characters, so count twice. The original's 126 are for one run; with the notebook's sweep over 150 parameter sets, 172 |
| One run | 3.0 s | 131 s | 667,864 agents for 3,650 days. LASER runs on all 20 cores; Starsim on one. Starsim's time includes building the sim |
| Results | | | **Agree.** Over the 10 years, 104,092 infections against 106,639 (means of 5 and 50 runs; four of the 5 Starsim runs are inside the original's 5% to 95% range of 103,139 to 108,758, and one is below it, at 101,352); in each of seven places from Norwich to Southwold within 3.8%; 529,399 people at the end against 529,833, and 43,114 deaths in the first year against 43,037. The share of weeks with no infections in each place, in the second half, correlates at 0.99 across places, but is a little lower on average (0.59 against 0.63) |

Measured on 14 October 2026 with Starsim 4.0.0 (the `v4` branch) on a 20-core x86-64 Linux machine.

## What was declared and what was not

Declared: the four states and three transitions, the latent period as an Erlang with 40 stages (the original's gamma with shape 40) and the infectious period as a normal, births into `S`, the places with their populations and the original's distance matrix, the coupling as a moving force of infection (`ss.Mobility(..., kind='force')`), the initial conditions in each place, the age pyramid, and the seasonal transmission as a time series that repeats every year.

Not declared, and marked as workarounds:

- **Deaths** (`lifespan`, 8 lines): a function that gives each person without one a time of death, drawn from the life table given their age, by writing `sim.people.ti_dead`.
- **Importation** (`importation`, 7 lines): a function that moves three random susceptible people to `I` every 30 days, by writing the disease's states.
- **The gravity matrix** (5 lines): built in NumPy and passed as `matrix=`, since its scaling cannot be given to `ss.gravity` and `ss.Mobility`.

## Findings

1. **Fixed: a stop of 1900 or more was read as a calendar year, silently (blocked agreement until worked around).** `ss.Sim(..., unit='day', stop=3650)` ran from 3649.86 to 3650 in 51 steps, not from day 0 to day 3,650, against the spec's rule that a bare number is a year only in a sim with dates. Repro: with `S, I, R = ss.states(3)` and `sim = ss.Sim(ss.Disease(infection=S >> I @ ss.perday(0.3)*I, recovery=I >> R @ ss.days(10), init=dict(I=10)), total_pop=1000, unit='day', stop=3650)`, after `sim.init()`, `sim.timevec` had 51 entries (with `stop=1899`, 1,900). Now, with `unit=` given (and not `'year'`), a bare `stop` is in that unit however large, and the model writes `stop=years*365`.
2. **Speed (blocks running the full model).** At full size (30 million people, 40 years) neither form of the Starsim model runs in a reasonable time. As agents, Starsim takes 0.33 s a step for 3 million agents (110 ns for each agent and step; a profile shows the time spread over grouping people by place, indexing, and counting), so 40 years at a tenth of the size would take 80 minutes, against 71 s for LASER. As compartments it cannot be written faithfully (the life table needs ages), and even with deaths at a constant rate, `solver='binomial'` takes 48 s a simulated year over the 954 places, because the latent period's 40 Erlang stages make it take 40 substeps a day. At the size used here Starsim is about 45 times slower than LASER on 20 cores. The implementation plan already lists compiled agent passes for exposure by place and for counts; for the binomial solver, a substep count set by the fastest stage rate rather than the number of stages (or exact Erlang shifts of the stage vector) would help (fewer substeps were tried, and made the stages about 25% too long, so this is an open item in the implementation plan).
3. **Deaths from a life table cannot be declared.** LASER draws each person's date of death once, from a life table, at the start (given their age) and at birth. `ss.death` takes a rate, or a dwell time that restarts whenever someone changes state, so neither does this. A first version used a hazard by year of age (`ss.death(rate=func)` with `sim.people.age`, as agents): it gave 29,800 deaths in the first year against 43,000, because LASER gives someone who is part way through a year of age the whole year's chance of dying in what is left of it. Writing `ppl.ti_dead` directly reproduces the original exactly (43,041 against 43,037), but `People.ti_dead` is documented as not for users. Suggested: `ss.death(age=ss.lifetable(ages, deaths))`, or `ss.People(lifespan=...)`, which draws an age at death for each person on creation, given their age, and schedules it.
4. **Importing a fixed number of people on a schedule cannot be declared.** The original moves exactly three susceptible people, chosen from the whole population, to `I` on days 30, 60, ..., 3,630. A transition takes a rate per person (the Ghana model wrote importation as a rate per susceptible), and a movement register moves people between places, not states. The function also had to set `ti_I` two days ahead, so that the imported people stay infectious for the original's 8 days rather than the 7 of the others (with `ti_I = ti`, the total was 2.9% low; with 8 days it agrees). Suggested: a seeding transition with a count and delivery keywords, e.g. `ss.seed('S -> I', n=3, interval=ss.days(30), stop=ss.days(3650), dur=ss.normal(8, 2))`, or `states=` and `to_states=` columns in a register.
5. **Fixed: no periodic time series.** The seasonality is 26 values, each held for 14 days of a year, repeated every year. `ss.seasonal` is a cosine, and `ss.timeseries` was not periodic, so the profile had to be written out for every year (260 entries). Now `ss.timeseries(..., period=ss.days(365))` repeats it, and the profile is given once (`period=ss.years(1)` also works; 365 days is LASER's year). The run with seed 0 gives exactly the same results as before (108,974 infections).
6. **The gravity model's scaling cannot be given.** The notebook scales the gravity matrix so that the mean share exported, over places, is `k`, and then caps each place at 0.2. `ss.Mobility(away=)` sets every place's share to the same value, and the only cap is 1. Also, `ss.gravity`'s exponent `a` is that of `pop[i]^(a−1)`, so LASER's `a=0` is Starsim's `a=1`, which is easy to get wrong. Here the matrix is built in NumPy. Suggested: `ss.Mobility(ss.gravity(b=0.25, c=1.56), kind='force', away=ss.mean(0.0446), cap=0.2)` or similar.
7. **`ss.gamma(40, 0.25)` is not a gamma with shape 40 and scale 0.25.** The second positional parameter is SciPy's `loc`, so this is a latent period of 40.25 days, and nothing warns. LASER (and NumPy) take `(shape, scale)`. `ss.erlang(k=40, mean=ss.days(10))` is used here, and is clearer. Suggested: make `loc` keyword-only, or warn when a dwell time has a nonzero `loc`.
8. **The original's infectious period is a day shorter than written (a finding about the original).** The notebook lists the components with `Exposed` before `Infectious`, so someone who becomes infectious counts down on the same day, and is infectious for `round(normal(8, 2)) − 1` days. The translation uses `normal(7, 2)`; with `normal(8, 2)` the reproduction number would be 31 rather than 27. The latent period is the same in both (someone infected on day `t` with a latent period of `m` days first infects others on day `t + m + 1`), which follows from Starsim's order within a step.
9. **Deaths outside a declared transition are not in the disease's results.** They are in `sim.results.new_deaths`, not `measles.results`, which is minor.
